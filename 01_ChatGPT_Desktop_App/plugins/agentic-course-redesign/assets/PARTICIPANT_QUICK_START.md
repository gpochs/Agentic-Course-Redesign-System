# Start a course redesign

Start with a sentence about what you teach and what you want your learners to do. For example, “My students can describe two theories but struggle to choose between them in a new case. Help me redesign one lesson.” You can also begin with an existing course, a single activity or a fictional example.

You can start with the model you already have open and keep it for the whole course. If you’d like help choosing, ask the plugin to suggest a model for each task, or choose as you go. You can change your mind later. If you ask for a plan, you’ll see which account each model uses, what your subscription covers and anything the plugin still needs to check. Once you agree, it will ask again before using a different account or sharing material outside that plan.

1. **Start with your course.** Choose any mix of a learning platform such as Moodle or OLAT, files on your computer or in the cloud, a learning app such as Kahoot!, Mentimeter, Klicker or H5P, and other sources such as a website, video or design reference. You can add a description, start with your description alone, or choose **Skip for now**. The check helps keep course and student information in the right place. Skipping takes you straight to the teaching goal. You can still design, produce and review new materials.
2. **Choose the materials we can use.** Describe the type before sharing a private link or file. Say which items may be read, who can see the results and whether the plugin may work in the chosen app. Different items can carry different permissions. If one is unavailable, work with the rest. When you want editable files, choose a dedicated course folder or private draft. A OneDrive folder syncs to the cloud.
3. **Check the course brief.** Correct a short summary of your learners, goals and constraints. An approved syllabus may already describe the programme, prerequisites or assessment. The plugin asks about missing details only when they would affect a decision. “I’m unsure” or “leave that open” are useful answers. Describe any access need in general terms, such as “the whole activity must work with a keyboard”.
4. **Choose what to focus on.** See where students practise the intended skill and what they are asked to do in the assessment. Say what matters most to you. You can keep the current approach, change the suggested focus or choose another.
5. **Choose which changes to make.** Review a concrete before-and-after example and its supporting evidence. Consider the time and work it would take. You can adopt one suggestion, adapt another and leave the rest.
6. **Review the plan and files.** Check the exact drafts, who will see them and what will change before creating them. If you have a design reference, identify what to preserve, such as the heading style and layout of your existing slides.
7. **Try the drafts.** Open an editable file or private draft. Compare the changed page, learning-outcomes slide or quiz question with its previous version. Try one action as a learner, such as following a handout link by keyboard or checking when quiz feedback appears. The plugin tells you which checks it performed and what it could not verify. If you want a learner’s perspective, you can invite someone to try the task and describe anonymously what happened. You can skip this and finish. If a draft no longer suits your class, say which one to leave out. For example, “Keep the worksheet and leave out the quiz.” If the worksheet still needs a change, you will see it again. When you are ready, confirm which drafts are finished, then decide where to save the handover.
8. **Decide what to use.** Review each item in the handover and choose what you will use, change or leave out. You might say, “I’ll use the handout. On slide four, give students time to answer before showing the graph.” A draft you leave out stays where it was. Anyone who already has access there keeps that access. If you leave everything out, you can stop. Publishing to learners is a separate decision.
9. **Finish or suggest an improvement.** You can finish here or ask for a written suggestion about the redesign process. If you say “Finish here”, the plugin confirms what you accepted and where you received it, then stops.
10. **Would you like a second opinion?** You can finish here, prepare a review yourself or ask the plugin to help find another reviewer. For an AI review, it first looks in the same app for a model from a family that didn’t help write the course, such as one from another developer. It can then check another app you already use. If every available family helped, you’ll see which helped least and what it did. You can accept that limited review or finish. Choose the drafts and check the summary of all nine steps. You can take them to a colleague yourself. If you want the plugin to send them, check the exact text, files, recipient, model and cost before giving the final go-ahead. You decide what to change.

The plugin shows one decision at a time. For a choice with defined answers, it uses a card when the app can show every option. If several answers can apply, you can select or type a combination. Use your own words for teaching priorities, trade-offs and feedback.

For an online course, open the specific course or draft in the in-app browser if available. Select the tab you want the plugin to use and sign in yourself. You can also use an authorised export or a description. If a copied course links to another quiz, app or resource, the plugin checks which copy will be edited. For a local file, ask to see the editable version or preview in the app that runs it.

If an optional tool would help with a particular task, the plugin explains what it adds, the material it would receive and any cost. For example, you can choose a writing skill for the student instructions in this batch of worksheets, ask for help finding it, or continue with the built-in editing tools. For a missing capability, you can ask the specialist to find a current candidate through its publisher or an official catalogue. The plugin can show who maintains it, what access it needs and how to try a fictional example. You decide separately whether to install it and what to share. You can change your mind before the next task.
